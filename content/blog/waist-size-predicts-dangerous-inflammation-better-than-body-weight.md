Among overweight women, whether their waist circumference crosses the metabolic risk threshold of 88cm predicts their inflammatory status better than their BMI category does. Those with high-risk waist circumference have a high-CRP prevalence of **27.1%**. Those with low-risk waist circumference: 4.4%. Same BMI band. A sixfold difference in inflammatory risk.

That gap is the core problem with relying on body weight alone.

## The Hidden Risk Inside "Normal" BMI

BMI is a blunt instrument. It captures total mass, not distribution. And for a meaningful share of adults who register as clinically normal, that distinction carries real consequences.

Among normal-BMI adults, 9.3% are women whose waist circumference exceeds the 88cm metabolic risk threshold. That group has a weighted mean hsCRP of 1.67 mg/L, compared to 1.43 mg/L for normal-BMI women with low-risk waist circumference. The absolute difference looks modest, but the prevalence of high-risk CRP (above 3 mg/L) tells a sharper story: **15.2%** for high-waist normal-BMI women versus 8.1% for their low-waist counterparts. Nearly double the rate of clinically elevated inflammation, in people a standard clinical intake would classify as healthy weight.

The pattern holds for men. Normal-BMI men with high-risk waist circumference show a weighted mean hsCRP of 3.22 mg/L and a high-CRP prevalence of 18%, compared to 2.01 mg/L and 11.3% for normal-BMI men with low-risk waist circumference. These are people who would not trigger a weight-based intervention in most clinical settings. Their inflammatory profiles suggest they should.

## Where the Risk Compounds

The most striking numbers come from the obese category, and they set a useful ceiling for understanding the gradient. Obese women with high-risk waist circumference have a weighted mean hsCRP of 6.83 mg/L and a high-CRP prevalence of **56.3%**. More than half of that group carries systemic inflammation at a level associated with elevated cardiovascular risk. For context, the clinical threshold for high CRP is 3 mg/L. Their average is more than double that.

But the more policy-relevant finding may be in the middle of the distribution, among adults who are overweight or normal-weight but carry abdominal fat above the risk threshold. Among adults aged 40 to 69 with high-risk waist circumference and BMI under 30, 35.9% have hypertension, 13.4% have diabetes, and 46.9% have high cholesterol. Those are not the numbers of a low-risk population. They are close to the figures for obese adults with high-risk waist circumference, who show 50.0% hypertension, 21.1% diabetes, and 49.9% high cholesterol.

The cardiometabolic burden in the non-obese, high-waist group is not dramatically lower than in the obese group. The difference is that the obese group is far more likely to be flagged, monitored, and treated.

## The Measurement That Gets Skipped

| Group | High-CRP Prevalence | Weighted Mean hsCRP |
|---|---|---|
| Obese women, high-risk waist | 56.3% | 6.83 mg/L |
| Overweight women, high-risk waist | 27.1% | 3.05 mg/L |
| Normal-BMI men, high-risk waist | 18.0% | 3.22 mg/L |
| Normal-BMI women, high-risk waist | 15.2% | 1.67 mg/L |
| Overweight women, low-risk waist | 4.4% | 1.83 mg/L |
| Normal-BMI women, low-risk waist | 8.1% | 1.43 mg/L |

The table above makes the waist-circumference gradient visible in a way BMI alone cannot. Overweight women with low-risk waist circumference have a high-CRP prevalence of 4.4%, lower than normal-BMI women with high-risk waist circumference at 15.2%. BMI rank and inflammatory risk rank are not the same list.

Waist circumference takes about ten seconds to measure. Among adults aged 40 to 69 with high-risk waist circumference but BMI under 30, 46.9% already have high cholesterol. What proportion of those individuals are currently undiagnosed or untreated, precisely because their BMI kept them off the clinical radar?
