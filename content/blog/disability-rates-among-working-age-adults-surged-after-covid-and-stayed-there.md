Cognitive disability among working-age Americans has risen steadily since 2019 and shows no sign of retreating. That's the clearest signal in five years of post-pandemic BRFSS data, and it runs in the opposite direction from what most people assume about COVID's lasting health effects.

The share of adults aged 18 to 64 reporting serious difficulty concentrating rose from **12.5% in 2019 to 15.2% in 2024**, a 2.7 percentage point increase that held firm across three consecutive survey years. For context, that same rate was 11% in 2014. A decade of gradual drift upward, then a sharp post-2020 acceleration that never reversed.

## Cognitive Disability Rose While Physical Disability Fell

The divergence between cognitive and physical disability trends is the most striking feature of the data. Serious difficulty walking among adults 18 to 64 was 10.5% in 2019 and dropped to 9.4% in both 2023 and 2024, continuing a long decline from 11% in 2014. Difficulty doing errands alone rose modestly, from 6.6% in 2019 to 7.2% in 2023 and 7.1% in 2024, compared to 6.1% in 2014.

So physical mobility limitations are, if anything, improving. Cognitive limitations are not. These two trends are moving in opposite directions within the same population over the same period. That gap matters for how employers structure accommodations, how disability insurers model risk, and how clinicians screen working-age patients who present with fatigue or memory complaints.

The demographic breakdown adds texture. White women aged 18 to 64 saw any-limitation prevalence rise from 24.2% in 2019 to **27.1% in 2023**, holding at 27.0% in 2024. White men rose from 21.1% to 22.8% over the same period. Among Black women, the pattern is different: prevalence was 28.2% in 2019, peaked at 29.6% in 2022, and fell back to 27.7% in 2024, suggesting the post-COVID increase may have been more transient in that group. Asian men saw a sharp rise from 11.0% in 2019 to 15.7% in 2023, then fell to 12.5% in 2024.

## The Cost Barrier Is Nearly Three Times Higher for People With Limitations

Having a functional limitation doesn't just affect what you can do. It affects whether you can afford care when you need it.

Among working-age adults with at least one functional limitation in 2022 to 2024, **26.1% reported being unable to see a doctor due to cost**. Among adults without limitations, that figure was 9.8%. Nearly triple the rate, for the group that needs care most.

What makes this gap particularly consequential is what doesn't differ: the share lacking a personal doctor was virtually identical across both groups, 20.7% for those with limitations and 20.9% for those without. Having a usual source of care isn't the problem. Affording to use it is.

That finding reframes the access question. The standard policy response to unmet medical need focuses on coverage and provider availability. But if people with disabilities have doctors at the same rate as everyone else and still can't afford visits at nearly three times the rate, the barrier is financial, not structural. A working-age adult managing a cognitive or physical limitation who skips appointments because of cost is likely to accumulate unmanaged conditions, which feeds back into workforce participation, disability insurance claims, and emergency utilization.

## A Sustained Shift, Not a Blip

The 2020 data showed a temporary dip in several disability measures, almost certainly a survey artifact from pandemic-era data collection disruptions. What followed is harder to explain away. Difficulty concentrating climbed from 12.5% in 2019 to 13.2% in 2021, 14.4% in 2022, 15.0% in 2023, and 15.2% in 2024. Each year higher than the last. No reversion.

A population-level shift of this magnitude, sustained across four consecutive survey years and showing up across multiple demographic groups, is not noise. Working-age adults are the backbone of the labor force and the primary contributors to employer-sponsored insurance pools. A persistent rise in cognitive disability in this group has downstream effects on productivity, absenteeism, and long-term disability claims that compound quietly until they don't.

What specific conditions or exposures are driving the divergence between cognitive and physical disability trends remains an open question the data can't answer on its own. But the trend line is clear enough: difficulty concentrating among working-age adults has climbed 4.2 percentage points since 2014, and nothing in the last five years suggests it has peaked.
