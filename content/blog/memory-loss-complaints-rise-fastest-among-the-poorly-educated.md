The title promises a story about memory loss rising fastest among the poorly educated. The data supports a different, and arguably more important, claim: the gap is large and persistent, but it isn't widening in any way the numbers can confirm.

Start with two figures. In 2019, **19.4%** of adults 45 and older who didn't finish high school reported worsening memory. Among college graduates, the figure was **7.5%**. That's a gap of 11.9 percentage points, and it was nearly as wide in 2015, when the rates were 18.2% and 7.1%.

Over those four years, the rate for the least educated rose 1.2 points. For college graduates it rose 0.4 points. Neither change is large. What stands out is how little the gap moved.

## A Gap That Holds in Every Year

Across the five survey years provided, adults without a high school diploma reported worsening memory at rates between 14.1% and 19.4%. College graduates stayed between 6.4% and 7.5%. The two ranges never touch.

| Year | Did not graduate HS | Graduated college |
|---|---|---|
| 2015 | 18.2% | 7.1% |
| 2016 | 18.8% | 6.4% |
| 2017 | 14.1% | 6.8% |
| 2018 | 16.9% | 7.4% |
| 2019 | 19.4% | 7.5% |

In 2016, the least educated group reported subjective memory decline at nearly three times the rate of college graduates (18.8% versus 6.4%). For a patient, that means a person without a diploma is far more likely to notice, and report, that their memory is getting worse. For a clinic or a screening program, it means the demand for cognitive evaluation is not distributed evenly across the education spectrum.

## The Middle Groups Barely Move

High school graduates reported worsening memory at 11.4% in 2015 and 12.3% in 2019. Adults who attended some college went from 11.3% to 10.8% over the same span. Both groups sat in a narrow band around 10% to 12% in every year.

So the pattern isn't a smooth gradient with each additional level of schooling shaving off a similar amount. The middle two groups are close to each other, and the real separation happens at the ends. By 2019, high school graduates (12.3%) and college attendees (10.8%) were 1.5 points apart. The distance from high school graduates to the no-diploma group was 7.1 points.

The practical consequence: broad education categories like "some college or less" would hide most of the disparity. The people reporting the most memory decline are concentrated in the group that never finished high school.

## The 2017 Dip Isn't a Finding

The no-diploma rate fell to 14.1% in 2017, then returned to 16.9% in 2018 and 19.4% in 2019. It's tempting to read that as a real improvement and rebound. The sample sizes argue against it.

The 2017 estimate rests on 1,811 respondents, compared with 9,742 in 2015 and 8,558 in 2019. The 2018 estimate is thinner still: just 479 adults who didn't finish high school. Estimates built on a few hundred respondents carry wide margins, and a 2.5-point swing in either direction wouldn't be surprising. The 2018 figure of 16.9% should be treated as a rough placeholder, not a data point to build an argument on.

The college-graduate series, by contrast, is stable even in the thin years (6.8% in 2017 on 6,470 respondents, 7.4% in 2018 on 3,372). That tells us the dip is specific to the smallest subgroup, which is what you'd expect from sampling noise rather than a genuine shift in how the least educated experience memory change.

The reliable years, 2015, 2016, and 2019, all land between 18.2% and 19.4%. That's the number to carry forward.

## What the Data Can and Can't Say About a Trend

The framing that memory complaints are rising fastest among the least educated is not something these numbers establish. A 1.2-point increase from 2015 to 2019 is real in the sense that the arithmetic holds, but it sits inside the noise of a survey whose middle years are unreliable for this group. Calling it a trend would overstate it.

What the data does establish is a disparity that was present at the start of the window and still present at the end, one that is large enough to matter for how cognitive health services get targeted. A rate of 19.4% means roughly one in five adults over 45 without a diploma reports their memory is getting worse. Among college graduates, it's closer to one in thirteen.

Whether that 14.1% reading in 2017 reflects anything beyond a small sample is the question the available years can't settle.
