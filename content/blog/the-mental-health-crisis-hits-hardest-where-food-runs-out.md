More than one in three adults who can't reliably put food on the table meets the clinical threshold for depression. That number, 36.5%, comes from NHANES data and it holds up even when you control for income.

That's the finding that should reframe how we think about mental health burden in the United States.

## A Dose-Response Relationship That's Hard to Ignore

The data across food security levels reads almost like a pharmacology chart. Full food security: mean PHQ-9 score of 3.33, with **8.5%** meeting the clinical threshold for depression (PHQ-9 ≥ 10). Marginal food security: 4.57 mean score, 15% above threshold. Low food security: 5.66 mean score, 21.9% above threshold. Very low food security: mean score of 7.93, with **36.5%** clinically depressed.

Each step down the food security ladder corresponds to a measurable increase in both average symptom burden and the share of people crossing into clinical territory. The gap between full and very low food security is not marginal. It's a 4.3x difference in depression prevalence. For context, that's a larger relative gap than most condition-specific risk factors that routinely drive clinical screening protocols.

What this means practically: roughly one in three adults experiencing very low food security is walking around with a level of depressive symptoms that would, in a clinical encounter, warrant a diagnosis and treatment plan. Most of them are not getting one.

## Income Explains Less Than You'd Expect

Here's where the data gets genuinely surprising. A reasonable assumption is that food insecurity and poverty are so tightly correlated that separating their effects on mental health is almost impossible. The stratified analysis suggests otherwise.

Among adults with full food security, poverty matters a lot. Those near or below the poverty line (PIR < 1.3) had a 14.9% depression rate, compared to 8.0% for those above it. That's nearly a doubling. Poverty, on its own, carries real mental health burden.

But at the very low food security level, the income split almost disappears. Depression prevalence was 37.1% for those near or below poverty and 35.8% for those above it, a difference of just 1.3 percentage points. Adults with very low food security who are above the poverty line are nearly as likely to be clinically depressed as those who are below it.

The same pattern holds at low food security: 21.4% depression among those near or below poverty, 21.9% among those above it. Essentially identical.

This is a meaningful divergence from what the full food security group shows. Once food insecurity reaches a certain severity, income stops being the dominant predictor of mental health status. Something about the experience of not having reliable access to food is doing independent work.

## What the Numbers Can't Settle

| Food Security Level | Mean PHQ-9 | % Clinically Depressed | PIR < 1.3 | PIR ≥ 1.3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full | 3.33 | 8.5% | 14.9% | 8.0% |
| Marginal | 4.57 | 15.0% | 18.5% | 15.4% |
| Low | 5.66 | 21.9% | 21.4% | 21.9% |
| Very Low | 7.93 | 36.5% | 37.1% | 35.8% |

The table above captures the core story: depression prevalence rises steadily with food insecurity severity, and by the time you reach very low food security, income no longer separates the depressed from the not-depressed.

What the data cannot tell us is whether food assistance interventions would reduce that 36.5% depression rate among very food-insecure adults, or whether the mental health burden at that level of deprivation is driven by factors that food access alone wouldn't address. The near-identical depression rates across income strata at very low food security suggest the relationship is not simply about having less money. But whether it's the chronic stress of food uncertainty, the nutritional deficits themselves, or something else entirely, the data is silent on mechanism.

What it is not silent on: 36.5% of a population meeting the clinical threshold for depression is a number that belongs in conversations about where mental health resources are most needed, and who is least likely to be reached by systems designed around diagnosis and treatment rather than the upstream conditions that precede both.
