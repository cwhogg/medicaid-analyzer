Medicare paid for **80,555** hip, femur, or pelvic fracture discharges in 2023. A decade earlier, the same DRG family accounted for 38,856. That's a 107.3% increase, a change of 41,699 discharges, and it means hospitals now handle more than twice the fracture volume they did in 2013.

Fractures are among the clearest physical markers of how often aging goes wrong. Each discharge is an older person who fell, broke something structural, and needed inpatient care. Doubling in ten years is a hospital capacity problem and a Medicare budget problem at the same time.

## Florida leads on volume, California on cost

Florida recorded **7,214** fracture-related Medicare discharges in 2023, ahead of California (5,489) and Texas (4,815). Florida's lead is the one population alone would predict: it has the country's highest concentration of older residents.

Then look at the money. California's 5,489 discharges generated **$87.8 million** in total Medicare payment. Florida's 7,214 discharges generated $73.7 million. California had 24% fewer discharges and paid about 19% more in total.

That inversion repeats elsewhere. Maryland logged 1,805 discharges and $33.3 million in Medicare payment. Pennsylvania logged 2,936 discharges and $31.9 million. Maryland treated 1,131 fewer patients and still cost Medicare more. A ranking by discharge count tells you where fractures happen. A ranking by payment tells you something different, and the two lists don't match.

For anyone building a state-level picture of fracture burden, that matters. Volume maps to demographics. Payment maps to something about how each state's hospitals are priced and paid.

## Small towns charge Medicare the most per fracture

Payment per discharge varies by hospital location, and the pattern runs against intuition. Metropolitan area core hospitals, the large urban centers, handled **71,943** discharges across 1,213 hospitals at a weighted average Medicare payment of **$12,379** per discharge. They carry nearly 90% of the volume and sit near the bottom on cost.

Small town core hospitals sit at the top. Their weighted average was **$16,866** per discharge, 36% above the metropolitan figure. Rural hospitals with primary flow to a tract outside an urbanized area came second at $14,811. The lowest figure in the table belongs to hospitals with secondary flow to a larger urbanized area, at $11,975.

The small town figure rests on thin data: 182 discharges at 12 hospitals, a single fracture DRG. The rural figure covers 148 discharges. Neither is a large sample, and a handful of complex cases could move either number. Even so, the direction is consistent across the less urban categories. Micropolitan core hospitals, with a much larger base of 4,568 discharges, averaged $14,137, still 14% above the metropolitan core.

The consequence is direct. An older adult who fractures a hip near home in a small town generates a larger Medicare payment than one treated in a major metro area, while the largest hospitals absorb the overwhelming share of the caseload. Payment and patient volume are pulling in opposite geographic directions.

## What the doubling means for hospitals

The 2013-to-2023 comparison in the data collapses into a single severity category, so it can't show whether the growth came from simpler or more complex cases. What it does show is the scale: **80,555** discharges, up from 38,856, in a DRG group that is nearly always an emergency admission. Hospitals can't schedule these cases or defer them.

Ten states in the 2023 data each recorded more than 2,000 fracture discharges, from Florida's 7,214 down to Ohio's 2,022. Each of those states needs orthopedic capacity, surgical staffing, and post-acute placement for a patient population that is growing faster than the hospitals serving it.

The open question sits in the payment gap. Small town hospitals receive $16,866 per fracture discharge and metropolitan hospitals receive $12,379, and the data can't say whether sicker patients, higher cost structures, or something else accounts for the difference.
