Psychiatry clinicians are telehealth-flagged at a rate of **45%**. Podiatry is at **1.5%**. That is a 30-fold gap between two specialties in the same Medicare directory. Now look at geography: the highest state, Massachusetts, reaches 28.5%, and the lowest states shown, Florida and New Mexico, sit at 16.6%. The entire state range fits comfortably inside the range between a mid-tier specialty and a top one.

If you're comparing states on telehealth adoption, specialty mix is doing a lot of work you may be attributing to place.

## Specialty draws the sharp lines

Three specialties clear 40%: psychiatry (45%, or 11,560 of 25,688 clinicians), neurology (42.3%, 7,546 of 17,825), and clinical psychology (40.3%, 10,408 of 25,835). These are fields where much of the work is conversation, history-taking, and medication management. None of them requires a table, a scope, or a scalpel.

At the other end, the procedural and hands-on fields barely register. Podiatry sits at 1.5% (230 of 15,597) and ophthalmology at 1.6% (291 of 17,801). Orthopedic surgery reaches 9.1%. Neurology, at 42.3%, is more than four times as likely to carry the flag as orthopedics.

The big primary care specialties land in the middle. Family practice is 24.8% flagged (23,174 of 93,366) and internal medicine is 24.2% (24,318 of 100,340). Nurse practitioners, the largest specialty in the directory at 253,746 clinicians, come in at 19.4% (49,302). Because nurse practitioners are so numerous, they contribute more flagged clinicians than psychiatry and neurology combined, even at less than half psychiatry's rate.

For a patient, this means the odds that a directory listing advertises telehealth depend far more on which kind of clinician they're looking for than on where they live. A rural patient seeking a psychiatrist has better odds of finding a telehealth-flagged option than an urban patient seeking a podiatrist.

## States cluster in a narrow band

State-level shares barely vary by comparison. Massachusetts leads at 28.5% (13,624 of 47,795), followed by DC at 26.3%. California, the largest state with 133,478 clinicians, sits at 22.8% (30,454). Florida and New Mexico anchor the low end shown at 16.6% each.

That's a spread of under 12 percentage points across the states shown, against a specialty spread of nearly 44 points. The geographic story looks dramatic on a map, but the underlying variation is modest next to what specialty produces.

A state with a heavy concentration of mental health and neurology clinicians will look telehealth-forward almost by construction. A state weighted toward surgery, anesthesiology, and emergency medicine (the latter two both at 2.2%) will look laggard regardless of its policy environment. Raw state rankings, without adjusting for who practices there, partly measure workforce composition.

## Variation inside a single specialty is still real

Specialty doesn't explain everything. Hold the specialty constant and geography reappears. Among internal medicine clinicians, Massachusetts reaches 42.3% (1,703 of 4,026). Kansas, at the bottom of the states listed, is at 25.2% (153 of 607). That's a 17-point gap within one specialty, larger than the entire spread of overall state shares.

Other states fall between those poles. DC internists are at 34.5%, Maryland at 33.5%, and Hawaii at 33.3%. California, despite its size, comes in at 27.7%, close to the middle of the pack.

This matters because it shows both effects are operating. Specialty sets the baseline, and state environment then moves clinicians up or down from it. Internal medicine in Massachusetts looks more like neurology nationally than like internal medicine in Kansas. Whatever pushes Massachusetts internists toward the flag is strong enough to nearly double the Kansas rate.

The directory flag can't say what that is. It records whether a clinician is marked as offering telehealth, not how often visits happen, which payers cover them, or whether the flag reflects an active service line or a checkbox ticked during enrollment.

## What the numbers leave open

For analysts, the practical implication is straightforward: standardize by specialty before ranking states. A comparison that skips this step will reward states for their workforce makeup. For anyone arguing over state telehealth policy, the specialty spread suggests the most important lever may be which clinical fields are able to use it at all.

The Massachusetts-Kansas gap among internists remains. If the same specialty, in the same federal directory, differs by 17 points across states, something beyond clinical fit is shaping who gets flagged.
