In every one of the 20 states analyzed, physician assistants outnumber surgical MD/DOs by at least 2.57 to 1. In North Carolina, that ratio reaches **3.6 to 1**. But 99.63% of those PAs are enrolled under the generic "Physician Assistant" specialty, not under any surgical category. Those two facts, taken at face value, don't fit together cleanly.

## The Ratio That Holds Across Every State, Regardless of Size

The consistency is the finding. North Carolina, with 52,922 total clinicians, has 6,948 PAs against 1,929 surgical MD/DOs. Idaho, with fewer than 10,000 total clinicians, has a PA-to-surgical MD/DO ratio of 3.41, the second highest in the dataset. New Hampshire, the lowest-ranked state in the analysis, still shows a ratio of 2.57. Not one of the 20 states analyzed shows surgical MD/DOs outnumbering PAs.

For patients in lower-density states, this matters in a concrete way. When the clinician most likely to be present in a rural surgical setting is a PA rather than a board-certified surgeon, the supervision structure, scope of practice, and liability framework all shift. That's not a hypothetical; it's the baseline condition across every state in this analysis.

Idaho's position is particularly striking. Among rural-proxy states with low total clinician counts, Idaho's PA-to-MD/DO ratio across all specialties is **0.384**, the highest in that group. Wyoming, by comparison, sits at 0.183. PAs represent 13.8% of all clinicians in Idaho. Alaska, with only 4,590 total clinicians, shows PAs at 10.9% of the workforce and a PA-to-surgical MD/DO ratio of 2.69. These are not marginal differences.

## What the Enrollment Data Actually Shows

Here's where the picture gets complicated. Across all 93,057 PAs in the dataset, 99.63% are enrolled under the primary specialty listed simply as "Physician Assistant." Only 6 PAs nationally are enrolled under General Surgery as a primary specialty. Orthopedic Surgery accounts for 19, Urology for 8.

Those numbers are not rounding errors. They represent the actual enrollment classifications used in the clinician directory. A PA working in a rural surgical practice, assisting in procedures, managing post-operative care, and functioning as the most accessible clinical contact for surgical patients, is almost certainly enrolled under the generic "Physician Assistant" designation rather than any surgical category.

This creates a measurement problem with real consequences. The PA-to-surgical MD/DO ratios that show 3.6 in North Carolina and 3.41 in Idaho are constructed by comparing PAs (almost none of whom are classified under surgical specialties) against MD/DOs who are explicitly enrolled in General Surgery, Orthopedic Surgery, or Urology. The ratio is real. What it measures is less certain.

## A Classification System That Obscures What It Should Reveal

The credentialing architecture here is doing something specific: it collapses nearly all PA activity into a single non-descriptive category while preserving surgical specialty distinctions for physicians. The result is a dataset that can tell you there are 3.6 times as many PAs as surgical MD/DOs in North Carolina, but cannot tell you how many of those PAs are actually participating in surgical care delivery.

For Medicare enrollment purposes, this matters because reimbursement, supervision requirements, and scope-of-practice rules all interact with how a clinician is classified. A PA enrolled under "Physician Assistant" in a rural surgical practice operates under the same enrollment category as a PA doing dermatology consultations in a suburban clinic. The directory treats them identically.

Rural patients receiving surgical care from Medicare-enrolled clinicians may have no way of knowing whether their provider's enrollment classification reflects their actual clinical role. The ratio of 3.6 PAs per surgical MD/DO in North Carolina could reflect genuine PA participation in surgical care delivery across a large, geographically dispersed state. Or it could reflect a credentialing classification artifact that makes PA surgical involvement invisible in the data while making PA numerical dominance appear absolute.

The dataset can confirm which number is larger. It cannot confirm what that number means.
