One thing needs flagging before the article: the title says hospital outpatient settings collect more, but the verified data shows the opposite in most of the codes examined. In this dataset, Office payments per service exceed Facility payments for CPT 17003, 88305, 99213, and 99214. The article below follows the data and addresses the title's premise directly.

---

The headline premise gets the direction wrong for most of these codes. For CPT 17003, destruction of precancer skin growths, Medicare paid an average of **$4.72** per service in the Office setting and **$1.43** in the Facility setting. That's a 230.3% difference, and the larger payment went to the office.

The gap is a matter of how Medicare's physician fee schedule works. In a facility, the hospital bills separately for overhead, so the physician's payment drops. In an office, the physician's payment absorbs the practice expense. The same procedure, performed by the same clinician, carries a different price tag depending on where the patient lies down.

## The same visit, priced two ways

Take the two most common established-patient visit codes. CPT 99213 (20-29 minutes) averaged $44.71 in Facility versus $59.70 in Office, a 33.6% gap. CPT 99214 (30-39 minutes) averaged $66.59 versus $84.26, a 26.5% gap, across 99,338,804 total services.

Those are the most heavily billed evaluation codes in the data. A 26.5% premium on a code billed nearly 100 million times is not a rounding error. It means the practice-expense component the office setting receives is large enough to move the total payment by roughly a quarter.

Pathology shows the largest gap among high-volume codes after 17003. CPT 88305, tissue examination of intermediate complexity, averaged $28.04 in Facility and $44.04 in Office, a 57.1% difference.

This payment is only half the picture, because it covers the professional claim alone. The hospital's separate facility fee does not appear in these figures. A patient seen in a hospital outpatient department generates two bills, and only one of them is measured here.

## Not every code splits by setting

The pattern is far from universal. CPT 80053, the comprehensive metabolic panel, paid $10.34 in Facility and $10.33 in Office. CPT 99233, a subsequent hospital care code requiring at least 50 minutes, ran the other direction: $93.62 in Facility versus $86.78 in Office, a -7.3% difference.

Codes that behave identically across settings tend to be those where no meaningful practice-expense difference exists, such as lab tests paid on a flat schedule. Codes that diverge are the ones where overhead is built into the payment. For policy analysts, the practical consequence is that any site-neutral payment proposal affects a narrow band of services, not the whole fee schedule.

Hospital-based care codes like 99233 also run the opposite way because the service itself is delivered in a facility. The patient is an inpatient, so the office rate has no real counterpart.

## The money has moved toward offices

Here is the part that complicates the framing. If facilities were collecting more per service, you would expect their share of payments to grow. It shrank.

Facility settings accounted for 38.29% of total Medicare payments in 2013 and 30.46% in 2023. Office share rose from 61.71% to 69.54% over the same period. In dollars, Facility payments fell slightly, from $29,400,888,941 to $28,544,528,063, while Office payments grew from $47,374,805,588 to $65,176,547,750.

The dollar figures matter more than the shares. Office payments increased by about $17.8 billion while facility payments declined by about $0.9 billion. Whatever drove spending growth over the decade, it landed overwhelmingly in the office setting, which also pays the higher rate on the codes examined above.

Some specialties moved the other way. Hematology's facility share rose from 17.4% to 22%, a 4.6 percentage point increase, the largest among specialties tracked. Surgical oncology (54.5% to 58.1%) and nephrology (37.6% to 40.8%) also shifted toward facilities. Dermatology went the opposite direction, falling from 3.3% to 1.8%.

Specialties that treat patients with infusions and complex drug regimens are the ones migrating toward hospital settings. Those are the same services where a hospital's separate facility fee, absent from these figures, can be largest.

## What the payment gap leaves out

The physician-fee-schedule data shows who gets paid what on the professional claim. It does not show total cost to Medicare or to the patient, which includes the hospital's facility fee and any associated coinsurance.

So the question the title poses cannot be answered from these numbers alone. The office pays the physician more for CPT 17003, yet Medicare may pay far more in total when a hospital adds its own bill. Why do some codes, like 17003, diverge by 230.3% across settings while others, like 80053, differ by a single cent?
