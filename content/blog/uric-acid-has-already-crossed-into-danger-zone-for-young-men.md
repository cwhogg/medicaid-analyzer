Men in their 30s now have higher rates of hyperuricemia than men over 60. That's not a typo.

**20.2% of men aged 30-39** already exceed the 7.0 mg/dL uric acid threshold that defines hyperuricemia, compared to just 13.1% of men 60 and older. The condition has long been associated with gout, kidney stones, and cardiovascular disease in older adults. Finding it more prevalent in men who are, statistically, decades from retirement age reframes what clinicians should be looking for during routine visits with young male patients.

## Young Men Are Crossing the Threshold Before They're Old Enough to Expect It

The pattern starts early. Among men aged 18-29, **16.8% already exceed 7.0 mg/dL**, with a weighted mean uric acid of 5.91 mg/dL. By their 30s, that prevalence climbs to 20.2%, with a mean of 6.03 mg/dL. Both groups sit well above the clinical threshold at the population average, meaning a substantial share of young men are carrying uric acid levels that, in older patients, would prompt a conversation about gout prophylaxis and cardiovascular risk.

This matters because hyperuricemia rarely announces itself. There's no pain, no swelling, no obvious symptom until a gout flare or a kidney stone forces the issue. A 28-year-old with a uric acid of 7.5 mg/dL is unlikely to be screened for it unless a clinician is specifically looking.

## The Cardiometabolic Picture Is Already Compounding

Among young men aged 18-39 with hyperuricemia, the comorbidity burden is striking. **61.7% are obese** (BMI at or above 30), compared to 28% of young men with normal uric acid levels. Average BMI in the hyperuricemia group is 32.4, versus 27.6 in the normal group.

The lipid picture is similarly divergent. Young men with hyperuricemia average 147.3 mg/dL in triglycerides, and 42.5% have high triglycerides (at or above 150 mg/dL). Among those with normal uric acid, only 16.1% cross that threshold. Elevated triglycerides in a man in his late 20s or early 30s carry long-term cardiovascular consequences that accumulate over decades, not years.

Taken across all risk factors, 89.9% of young men with hyperuricemia have at least one cardiometabolic risk factor, compared to 82.9% of those with normal uric acid. The gap is real, but the baseline is also high: even among young men without hyperuricemia, more than four in five already carry some form of cardiometabolic risk. That context makes the hyperuricemia group's profile harder to dismiss as an outlier.

## Racial Disparities Add Another Layer

The distribution of elevated uric acid isn't uniform across racial and ethnic groups. Non-Hispanic Black men aged 18-49 have the highest prevalence of uric acid above 7.0 mg/dL at **22.7%**, compared to 17.0% among Non-Hispanic White men in the same age range. Mexican American men have the highest weighted mean uric acid at 6.09 mg/dL, with 16.8% above the threshold.

These differences matter for screening. If clinical attention to uric acid in young men remains low overall, it will be lowest in the populations where the burden is highest. Non-Hispanic Black men already face disproportionate rates of hypertension and chronic kidney disease, both of which interact with uric acid metabolism. A 22.7% hyperuricemia prevalence in that group, concentrated in men under 50, represents a compounding risk that standard cardiovascular screening panels may not capture.

Gout was once called the disease of kings, a condition of excess that arrived in old age after decades of rich food and wine. The NHANES data describes something different: a condition already present in roughly one in five men before they turn 40, clustering with obesity, elevated triglycerides, and other cardiometabolic markers in a population that isn't traditionally screened for it. Given that 89.9% of young men with hyperuricemia already carry at least one cardiometabolic risk factor, the question clinicians can't yet answer from this data is whether the uric acid is driving that burden or simply arriving alongside it.
