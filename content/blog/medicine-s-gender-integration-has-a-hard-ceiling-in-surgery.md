Even among doctors who finished their training after 2010, orthopedic surgery is **86.3% male**. That's not a historical artifact. That's the current pipeline.

General Surgery, by contrast, reached 43.2% female among post-2010 graduates, up from 14.8% among those who trained before 2000. A 28.4 percentage point shift across cohorts is real progress. Orthopedic surgery moved from 3.9% to 13.7% over the same span. Progress, yes. But the gap between these two surgical fields, trained in the same era, is now wider than the gap between orthopedics and where General Surgery started.

## Where Integration Actually Happened

The feminization of medicine's non-procedural workforce is nearly complete. Certified Nurse Midwives are 99.4% female. Nurse Practitioners, the largest clinical workforce in the dataset at 253,746 clinicians, are 87.3% female. Speech pathology, dietetics, social work, mental health counseling: all above 78% female. These are also, broadly, the lower-paying roles in the clinical hierarchy.

Even within surgery, the pattern holds in a specific way. Obstetrics/Gynecology is 69.6% female, with 21,845 female clinicians out of 31,401 total. Gynecological Oncology, a demanding surgical subspecialty, is 56.3% female. Both fields center on female patients and female reproductive health. The surgical specialties that have integrated most fully are the ones that were, in some sense, already coded as female territory.

## The Orthopedic Exception Isn't a Rounding Error

Urology offers a useful comparison. Among post-2010 graduates, urology is 24.6% female, up from 5.5% among pre-2000 graduates. That's a fourfold increase. Orthopedic surgery also roughly tripled its female share across cohorts, from 3.9% to 13.7%. But the absolute numbers tell the real story: among post-2010 orthopedic graduates, **6,099 are male and only 971 are female** out of 7,070 total clinicians. That's not a field in transition. That's a field that has absorbed a generation of post-integration medical school classes and emerged nearly unchanged in its gender composition.

The contrast with General Surgery is stark enough to demand attention. Both are high-acuity, procedurally intensive surgical fields. Both require long residencies and demanding call schedules. Yet General Surgery is approaching gender parity among recent graduates while orthopedics remains further from it than urology, a field that was 94.5% male among its oldest cohort.

## What the Pipeline Tells Us About the Ceiling

Medicine's integration story is real, but it has a specific shape. Women now make up large majorities of the clinical workforce in fields that are lower-paying, lower-prestige, or historically associated with caregiving. In surgery, integration has proceeded furthest in fields tied to women's health. The highest-earning, highest-status procedural specialties, orthopedic surgery chief among them, have moved the least.

This matters for patients. Orthopedic surgery is one of the most common and costly categories of care in the U.S. healthcare system. A workforce that is 86.3% male among its newest graduates will, for decades, be the one making decisions about pain management, surgical candidacy, and treatment protocols for a patient population that is roughly half female. Research consistently links patient-physician gender concordance to differences in communication, trust, and treatment outcomes.

General Surgery reached 43.2% female among post-2010 graduates. Orthopedic surgery, in the same cohort, sits at 13.7%. If the same integration trajectory that reshaped General Surgery over the past two decades had applied to orthopedics, the field would look fundamentally different today. It doesn't. And the gap between what's possible, as demonstrated by adjacent surgical fields, and what's actually happening in orthopedics is the number that should be driving the conversation.
