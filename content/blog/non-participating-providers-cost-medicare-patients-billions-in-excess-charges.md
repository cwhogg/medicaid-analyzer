Non-participating providers in Medicare are a small minority, but their concentration in specific specialties creates an outsized burden for patients who often don't know they're exposed until the bill arrives.

## Chiropractic Carries the Heaviest Non-Participation Load

No specialty comes close to chiropractic's opt-out rate. Of 53,030 chiropractors in Medicare, **3,413 are non-participating**, a 6.44% non-participation rate that leads every specialty in the data. The next closest is Osteopathic Manipulative Medicine at 2.42%, with 34 non-participating providers out of 1,405 total.

That gap matters because chiropractic is a high-volume, repeat-visit specialty. Medicare patients with chronic back pain or musculoskeletal conditions may see a chiropractor dozens of times per year. Each visit with a non-participating provider can expose them to balance billing above Medicare's approved amount, and many patients in rural or underserved areas have limited ability to shop for a participating alternative.

The charge-to-payment ratio for non-participating chiropractors is 1.68, compared to 2.00 for participating chiropractors. That smaller ratio gap, relative to other specialties, suggests non-participating chiropractors are submitting charges closer to what Medicare actually pays, but patients are still absorbing costs that participating providers cannot legally pass on.

## The Preventive Medicine Gap Signals a Structural Problem

The most striking ratio disparity in the data belongs to Preventive Medicine. Non-participating providers in that specialty show a charge-to-payment ratio of just 1.59, while participating providers carry a ratio of **3.50**, a gap of 1.91 points. That's the largest divergence in the dataset.

A higher charge-to-payment ratio for participating providers typically reflects higher submitted charges relative to what Medicare pays, not necessarily higher patient costs. But for non-participating providers, the lower ratio combined with opt-out status means patients face a different exposure: they may pay more out of pocket because the provider isn't bound by Medicare's limiting charge rules in the same way.

Ophthalmology and Psychiatry show similar patterns. Ophthalmology's non-participating providers have a charge-to-payment ratio of 1.57 versus 2.91 for participating providers, with 104 non-participating providers out of 23,072 total. Psychiatry has 270 non-participating providers out of 36,694 (0.74%), with a non-participating ratio of 1.58 against 2.61 for participating providers. Both are specialties where Medicare patients often have few substitutes and high ongoing need.

## Geography Concentrates the Risk

State-level data from 2013 shows that non-participation isn't evenly distributed. The Northern Mariana Islands had the highest non-participating service share at **0.59%**, with 71 non-participating services out of 11,981 total individual services. Arizona had 137 non-participating individual providers delivering 32,575 services out of 33,556,411 total (0.10% share), the highest absolute provider count among states with elevated non-participation rates.

Washington, D.C. came in at 0.11% non-participating service share. These numbers are small in percentage terms, but they're not random. Territories and states with thinner provider networks give patients less ability to avoid non-participating providers, which means the financial exposure is less a matter of patient choice and more a function of geography.

In Allergy/Immunology, non-participating providers account for just 0.11% of total services and 0.11% of total Medicare payments within the specialty. That near-zero share illustrates how concentrated the non-participation problem really is: a handful of specialties, particularly chiropractic, carry the bulk of the risk, while most of Medicare operates with near-complete participation.

What the data can't resolve is whether the lower charge-to-payment ratios seen among non-participating providers across specialties (ranging from 1.57 to 1.72, compared to 2.00 to 3.50 for participating providers) reflect genuinely lower submitted charges, or whether participating providers have simply learned to submit higher charges against a fixed Medicare allowed amount. That distinction determines whether patients facing non-participating providers are actually paying more in absolute dollars, or just more relative to what Medicare covers, and the answer would reshape how analysts measure the true cost of non-participation to beneficiaries.
