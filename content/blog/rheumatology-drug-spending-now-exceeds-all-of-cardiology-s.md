Medicare is spending more per prescriber on rheumatology drugs than on any other major specialty. By a lot.

In 2023, the average rheumatology prescriber generated **$1,522,273** in total drug costs. Cardiology's equivalent figure was $639,027. That's not a rounding error. Rheumatology's cost per prescriber was nearly 2.4 times higher than cardiology's, despite cardiology treating the leading cause of death in the United States.

## Fewer Doctors, Fewer Claims, Vastly Higher Costs Per Prescription

The scale difference between these two specialties is striking. Cardiology had 18,713 prescribers in 2023 and generated $11.96 billion in total drug costs across 61.3 million claims. Rheumatology had 5,118 prescribers and generated $7.79 billion across just 10.5 million claims.

| Specialty | Prescribers | Total Claims | Total Drug Cost | Cost Per Claim |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Family Practice | 103,916 | 356.8M | $29.5B | $82.66 |
| Internal Medicine | 102,764 | 324.9M | $34.0B | $104.61 |
| Cardiology | 18,713 | 61.3M | $12.0B | $194.93 |
| Rheumatology | 5,118 | 10.5M | $7.8B | $742.19 |

Rheumatology's **cost per claim of $742.19** is 3.8 times cardiology's $194.93, and nearly nine times family practice's $82.66. Every rheumatology prescription written under Medicare Part D costs, on average, more than a week of a typical American's wages. For payers building drug budgets around claim volume, this specialty is a systematic blind spot.

The reason is biologics. Rheumatology relies on a class of drugs, including TNF inhibitors and IL-17 blockers, that are among the most expensive in medicine. These aren't outliers in the specialty's prescribing mix. They are the prescribing mix.

## A Decade of Biologic Cost Growth With No Sign of Reversal

Tracked rheumatology biologics cost Medicare $1.75 billion in 2013. By 2023, that figure reached **$8.22 billion**, a nearly fivefold increase over ten years. The number of prescribers writing these drugs grew from 10,797 to 16,240 over the same period, a 50% increase. But prescriber growth alone doesn't explain the cost trajectory.

Cost per beneficiary tells the more consequential story. In 2013, the average beneficiary receiving a tracked rheumatology biologic cost Medicare $38,558.69 annually. By 2023, that figure was $185,527.75. The per-patient cost grew by more than $146,000 in a decade.

From 2021 to 2023, cost per beneficiary held relatively flat, moving from roughly $184,617 to $185,527. Whether that plateau holds depends on forces that are still playing out in the market, including biosimilar uptake for adalimumab, which lost its U.S. exclusivity in 2023. If biosimilar competition compresses per-unit costs, the per-beneficiary figure could fall. If uptake remains slow, the plateau may simply be a pause.

## The Cardiology Comparison Understates the Shift

Cardiology does have its own ultra-expensive drugs. Tafamidis Meglumine, used for transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy, had an average cost per beneficiary of $2,117,467.63 in 2023. Rheumatology's highest, Immun Glob G(Igg)/Pro/Iga 0-50, reached $1,954,389.50. At the extreme tail, the two specialties look similar.

But those are rare drugs treating rare conditions. The bulk of rheumatology's $7.79 billion comes from biologics prescribed at scale to hundreds of thousands of patients with rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, and ankylosing spondylitis. The cost concentration in rheumatology isn't a tail phenomenon. It's the center of the distribution.

Cardiology still spends more in absolute terms, $11.96 billion versus $7.79 billion. But cardiology achieves that with 18,713 prescribers writing 61.3 million claims. Rheumatology generates two-thirds of cardiology's total spend with 27% of the prescribers and 17% of the claims. On a per-prescriber and per-claim basis, rheumatology has already crossed cardiology. On a total-spend basis, at current growth rates, the gap closes within a few years.

The question that the 2021-2023 plateau raises is whether $185,000 per biologic beneficiary represents a ceiling or a temporary pause before the next generation of targeted therapies resets the baseline upward again.
