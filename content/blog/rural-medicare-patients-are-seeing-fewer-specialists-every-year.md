Rural cardiac surgery in Medicare has effectively ceased to exist outside cities. Thirteen providers billed Medicare for cardiac surgery in rural areas in 2013. By 2023, that number had fallen to 5. Across the country.

That single data point captures something broader happening across specialist medicine: rural Medicare patients are losing access to specialty care, and the remaining providers are being paid less for it.

## The Provider Collapse Is Uneven, But the Direction Is Consistent

Not every specialty has lost providers at the same rate. Neurology actually added three rural providers over the decade, going from 18 to 21. Orthopedic surgery held nearly flat, 76 providers in 2013 and 75 in 2023. But these numbers are small enough that "holding steady" is cold comfort. Twenty-one rural neurologists serving Medicare patients nationally means that in most rural counties, the nearest neurologist is hours away, if accessible at all.

Other specialties moved in the wrong direction. Rural anesthesiology providers declined from 320 in 2013 to 255 in 2023, a drop that affects not just anesthesia access but the viability of any surgical program that depends on it. Certified Clinical Nurse Specialists in rural areas fell from 46 to 30. Rural cardiology providers grew modestly, from 41 to 52, but that number remains almost invisible against the metropolitan count of **17,787** cardiology providers in 2023.

For rural seniors, fewer providers means longer travel times, delayed diagnoses, and care that gets deferred until it becomes an emergency. That's not a projection. It's the arithmetic of 52 cardiologists serving a geographically dispersed rural Medicare population.

## Metropolitan Providers Are Getting Paid More. Rural Ones Aren't.

The payment gap between rural and metropolitan specialists is persistent and, in some cases, widening. In 2023, rural cardiologists received an average Medicare standardized payment of **$60.16 per service**, compared to $87.33 for metropolitan cardiologists. That's a 31% gap. Rural neurologists received $76.27 per service versus $97.33 in metropolitan areas. Rural orthopedic surgeons received $135.93 per service, while their metropolitan counterparts received $153.89.

| Specialty | Rural Avg Payment | Metro Avg Payment | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cardiology | $60.16 | $87.33 | 31% |
| Neurology | $76.27 | $97.33 | 22% |
| Orthopedic Surgery | $135.93 | $153.89 | 12% |

These gaps exist even after Medicare's standardization process, which is designed to remove geographic cost-of-living differences. What remains is a structural payment differential that makes rural practice less financially viable, specialty by specialty.

Rural orthopedic surgery is a useful case study. The average payment per service actually fell from $140.49 in 2013 to $135.93 in 2023, even as metropolitan orthopedic payments rose from $140.27 to $153.89. In 2013, rural and metropolitan orthopedic payments were nearly identical. A decade later, a $17.96 gap has opened. The provider count in rural areas barely moved, but the financial incentive to stay has eroded.

## Volume Is Rising. Providers Aren't.

The most striking tension in this data involves rural cardiology. Despite having only 52 providers in 2023, rural cardiologists billed Medicare for **175,632 total services** that year, up from 111,851 services delivered by 41 providers in 2013. Services grew 57% while the provider count grew 27%.

Meanwhile, metropolitan cardiology moved in the opposite direction. Metropolitan providers fell from 20,725 to 17,787, and total metropolitan cardiology services dropped from roughly 77.9 million to 52.4 million. Urban cardiologists are seeing fewer patients. Rural cardiologists are seeing more.

Rural neurology tells a different story. Total rural neurology services fell from 16,563 in 2013 to 12,376 in 2023, even as metropolitan neurology services grew from 29.1 million to 41.0 million. Rural Medicare patients aren't just losing neurologists. They're receiving fewer neurology services overall, which suggests unmet need rather than efficient care.

The 52 rural cardiologists absorbing a growing patient load while being paid 31% less per service than their metropolitan peers are operating under conditions that, if sustained, will make rural cardiology recruitment harder, not easier. The question that the data raises but cannot answer: at what point does the patient volume become unsustainable for the providers who remain?
