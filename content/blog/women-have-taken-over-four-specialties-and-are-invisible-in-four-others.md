Women hold **87.3%** of nurse practitioner roles and just 14% of surgical positions among clinicians who trained before 1990. Those two numbers describe the same profession, separated by about 30 years.

The U.S. clinician workforce is not uniformly feminizing. It's bifurcating, with some specialties so thoroughly female they've effectively closed to men, while others are only now crossing parity for the first time. The dividing line, historically, has been the scalpel.

## The Specialties Women Already Own

Four specialties have crossed into territory that's hard to call anything other than female-dominated. Certified Nurse Midwives are **99.4% female** across 4,226 Medicare-enrolled providers, with only 22 male practitioners nationally. At least 14 states report 100% female CNM workforces, including Ohio (245 providers), New York (320 providers), and Minnesota (170 providers). Michigan's 130 CNMs are entirely female, a gender gap of 100 percentage points.

Nurse Practitioners are the largest majority-female specialty by raw headcount: 221,438 women out of 253,746 total enrolled providers, or 87.3%. Clinical Social Workers sit at 83.1% female across 58,710 providers, and Mental Health Counselors at 79.3% across 37,430. Physician Assistants, once a male-dominated field, are now 71% female, with 88,731 women versus 36,034 men among 124,919 enrolled providers.

The concentration matters for access. These four specialties collectively represent the front lines of behavioral health, primary care, and maternal care. A workforce that is 87% female in NP roles and 99% female in midwifery is not just a demographic fact. It means the pipeline for these roles is functionally single-sex, which creates fragility if recruitment patterns ever shift.

## The Surgical Floor That's Finally Moving

The pre-1990 surgical cohort tells a story of near-total exclusion: only 14% of surgical specialists who graduated before 1990 are women, or 2,189 out of 15,653 enrolled providers. Primary care wasn't much better in that era, at 23.8% female (8,262 of 34,751).

Among clinicians who graduated after 2010, both numbers have reached **50.5%**. Post-2010 surgical graduates are 14,906 women out of 29,533 total. Post-2010 primary care graduates are 39,470 women out of 78,082. The gap between the two specialty groups has essentially closed within a single generation.

| Cohort | Specialty Group | Total | Female | % Female |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Before 1990 | Surgical | 15,653 | 2,189 | 14.0% |
| Before 1990 | Primary Care | 34,751 | 8,262 | 23.8% |
| After 2010 | Surgical | 29,533 | 14,906 | 50.5% |
| After 2010 | Primary Care | 78,082 | 39,470 | 50.5% |

That convergence at exactly 50.5% across both specialty groups in the post-2010 cohort is striking. Surgical training, long the most resistant to gender integration, has reached statistical parity in its newest graduates. Within a decade, as the pre-1990 cohort retires, the aggregate female share of surgical specialties will look nothing like the number that currently defines it.

## Where the Ceiling Still Shows Up in the Aggregate

OB/GYN is 69.6% female among 31,401 enrolled providers, with 21,845 women versus 9,556 men. That's a majority-female specialty, but the aggregate number is pulled down by older male practitioners who trained when the field skewed male. Certified Registered Nurse Anesthetists sit at 60.3% female, the lowest share among the clearly majority-female specialties.

The aggregate figures for surgical specialties overall still reflect decades of male-dominated training classes. The pre-1990 cohort's 14% female share represents thousands of active providers who will remain in the workforce for years. That's why the overall surgical gender gap persists even as the incoming cohort has reached parity.

The structural shift is already locked in. Post-2010 graduates are 50.5% female in surgical fields, and that cohort will grow as a share of total enrolled providers every year. The question workforce planners haven't answered yet is which specific surgical sub-specialties are driving that parity, and which remain outliers still closer to the 14% baseline of the pre-1990 era.
