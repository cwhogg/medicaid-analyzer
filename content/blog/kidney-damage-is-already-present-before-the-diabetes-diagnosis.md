Kidney damage shows up in 15% of prediabetic adults aged 60 and older. That number, drawn from NHANES data, is nearly 50% higher than the 10.2% rate seen in their peers with normal blood sugar. The clinical threshold for diabetes hasn't been crossed. The kidney damage is already there.

## Albuminuria Doesn't Wait for a Diagnosis

Albuminuria, the presence of excess protein in urine, is one of the earliest measurable signs of kidney stress. A urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio at or above 30 mg/g is the standard clinical marker. By that measure, kidney damage is detectable well before HbA1c reaches the 6.5% threshold that triggers a diabetes diagnosis.

The pattern holds across every age group. Among adults aged 20 to 39, albuminuria prevalence was 6.4% in those with prediabetes compared to 3.6% in those with normal HbA1c. In the 40 to 59 age group, the gap was 7.9% versus 5.0%. By age 60 and older, **15.0% of prediabetic adults showed albuminuria**, against 10.2% in the normal group. Each age band shows the same directional relationship: prediabetes correlates with meaningfully higher kidney stress, even when the person has never received a diabetes diagnosis.

For patients, this means years of subclinical kidney damage may accumulate before any clinical intervention is triggered. For health systems, it means the standard screening protocol, which typically intensifies kidney monitoring after a diabetes diagnosis, may be arriving too late for a substantial share of patients.

## The Racial Gap Is Steeper Than the Age Gap

Race and ethnicity stratify the albuminuria burden in ways that don't map neatly onto the age findings. Non-Hispanic Asian adults with prediabetes had an albuminuria prevalence of 12.8%, more than double the 6.0% seen in their peers with normal HbA1c. Non-Hispanic Black adults showed a similar pattern: 12.9% with prediabetes versus 9.2% in the normal group. Non-Hispanic White adults with prediabetes came in at 10.9%, compared to 5.3% in the normal group.

The sharpest contrast appeared in the Other/Multi-Racial category. **Albuminuria prevalence reached 14.7% among prediabetic adults in this group**, compared to just 2.5% in those with normal HbA1c. That's a nearly sixfold difference, though the sample size (96 prediabetic adults) warrants some caution in how far to generalize.

These gaps matter because they suggest that a uniform screening threshold applied across all prediabetic adults will systematically underserve populations where kidney damage accumulates faster relative to glycemic status.

## Near-Poverty Patients Carry the Highest Kidney Burden

Income adds another layer. Among prediabetic adults with insurance and an income-to-poverty ratio between 1.0 and 1.99 (the "near poverty" band), the weighted mean UACR was **36.4 mg/g**, the highest of any income-insurance subgroup. That's nearly three times the 13.6 mg/g seen in higher-income insured prediabetic adults, and it exceeds the 23.4 mg/g recorded among insured adults below the poverty line.

The near-poverty insured group occupies a particular position: they have enough income to lose Medicaid eligibility in many states but not enough to buffer the health consequences of economic stress. Their UACR numbers reflect that exposure.

One finding cuts against the expected pattern. Adults with both prediabetes and albuminuria had the lowest rate of being uninsured or lacking routine care, at 9.7%, compared to 21.7% among those with normal HbA1c and normal kidney markers. The prediabetes-only group (no albuminuria) sat in between at 17.9%. Put differently, the sickest prediabetic patients are the most connected to the healthcare system.

Whether that connection reflects earlier detection made possible by routine care, or whether it reflects something about who gets tested and diagnosed in the first place, the data can't fully resolve. But the implication is uncomfortable either way: if greater healthcare access is what surfaces albuminuria in prediabetic patients, then the 15% prevalence figure in older adults may be an undercount among those with the least access.
