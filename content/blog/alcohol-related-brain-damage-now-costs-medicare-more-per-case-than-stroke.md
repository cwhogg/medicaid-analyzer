Fewer patients, higher bills. Average Medicare payment per discharge for alcohol-related neurological conditions reached **$11,701 in 2023**, up from $7,730 in 2013. Over that same decade, total alcohol-related discharges fell from 77,277 to 43,787, a drop of more than 43%. The cost per case is rising even as the patient population shrinks, and the gap between alcohol-related brain damage and stroke, once wide, has nearly closed.

## A Cost Trajectory That Outpaced Stroke

In 2021, average Medicare payment per discharge for stroke and intracranial DRGs was $13,755. Alcohol-related DRGs came in at $10,984 that same year. By 2023, alcohol-related discharges averaged $11,701 per case. The stroke figure for 2021 is the most recent available for direct comparison, but the trajectory is clear: alcohol-related neurological cases have closed a gap that once seemed fixed.

For context, the 2021 stroke average of $9,447 (for the narrower stroke-only DRG grouping, excluding intracranial) was already below the 2023 alcohol-related average. Alcohol-related brain damage now costs Medicare more per discharge than a standard stroke admission. That's a reversal of the conventional assumption that stroke, with its acute interventions and imaging costs, would always carry the heavier per-case price tag.

What this means in practice: Medicare is paying more per alcohol-related hospitalization than it did a decade ago, even with fewer patients coming through the door. If the remaining cases are more clinically severe on average, the cost trajectory will not flatten on its own.

## The Out-of-Pocket Burden Is Growing Too

Patients aren't insulated from this shift. Average out-of-pocket cost per discharge for alcohol-related neurological DRGs rose from $1,626 in 2013 to **$2,856 in 2023**, a 76% increase over the decade. Out-of-pocket costs as a share of total payment grew from 17.7% to 20.0% over the same period.

By comparison, the 2021 stroke average out-of-pocket cost was $2,227 per discharge. Alcohol-related neurological patients in 2023 paid more out of pocket per admission than stroke patients did two years earlier. For a Medicare population that often has fixed incomes and limited supplemental coverage, a $2,856 cost-sharing burden per hospitalization is not trivial.

## California Leads in Volume, Massachusetts in Relative Burden

State-level spending in 2023 shows two distinct patterns. California had the highest total Medicare spending on alcohol-related DRGs at $212,197,739, driven by 11,088 discharges. California also spent $196,249,162 on stroke DRGs, covering 14,901 discharges. Alcohol spending exceeded stroke spending in California despite alcohol cases representing fewer than half the discharge volume, which reflects the higher per-case cost directly.

Massachusetts tells a different story. With an alcohol-to-stroke spending ratio of 1.65, it showed the highest such ratio among reported states. Massachusetts spent $94,891,236 on alcohol-related DRGs versus $57,432,203 on stroke DRGs in 2023. Texas, by contrast, had a ratio of 0.94, meaning stroke spending still exceeded alcohol spending there. New York ($139,931,998) and Florida ($130,291,231) rounded out the top states by total alcohol-related Medicare spending.

The Massachusetts figure is the one that should give analysts pause. A state spending 65% more on alcohol-related neurological hospitalizations than on stroke is not a rounding error. It reflects a genuine concentration of high-cost cases, and it suggests that national averages may be masking significant regional variation in how this condition is accumulating costs.

Across the decade, total alcohol-related discharges fell by more than 43% while average Medicare payment per discharge rose by more than 51%. Whether the cases that remain are more complex, more resource-intensive, or simply more expensive to treat in a higher-cost inpatient environment, the per-case cost is now in the same range as one of Medicare's most recognized and heavily studied neurological conditions. The volume story says this problem is getting smaller. The cost story says it isn't.
