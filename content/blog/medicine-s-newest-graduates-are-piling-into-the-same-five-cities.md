Nearly one in three clinicians who graduated in 2015 or later is a Nurse Practitioner. That single fact reshapes every assumption about where the healthcare workforce is heading, and it collides directly with where that workforce is actually landing.

## The Five Cities Absorbing the New Workforce

New York City has **8,977 clinicians** from the post-2015 graduate cohort, representing 1.1% of all recent graduates nationally. Chicago follows at 7,018 (0.86%), then Houston at 6,381 (0.78%), with Boston and Dallas essentially tied at 5,063 and 5,040 respectively. Together, these five metros are pulling in a disproportionate share of every new clinician entering practice.

The concentration isn't just large in absolute terms. It's growing. Chicago's share of recent graduates (0.86%) is 0.26 percentage points above its share of the 1990-2000 graduate cohort (0.60%), the largest city-level increase in the dataset. Miami shows a similar pattern, rising 0.20 percentage points from 0.39% to 0.59%. These aren't cities that were already dominant and stayed dominant. They're cities that are pulling in a larger fraction of each successive graduating class. For patients in rural areas or mid-size metros, that trajectory matters more than the raw counts.

Houston is the notable exception. Its share barely moved, rising just 0.03 percentage points from the earlier cohort. That suggests the city's growth in absolute clinician numbers tracks roughly with national workforce growth rather than outpacing it.

## Puerto Rico and the States Being Left Behind

At the other end of the distribution, Puerto Rico has the lowest share of recent graduates among all states and territories: only **15.96%** of its 5,683 total clinicians graduated in 2015 or later. The national picture is one of a workforce that is aging in place in some regions while concentrating its newest entrants in a handful of major metros.

Hawaii (30.3%) and Vermont (33.14%) also rank near the bottom among the 50 states, suggesting that geographically isolated or lower-density markets are not attracting new graduates at the same rate as urban centers. A healthcare system that relies on its existing workforce without replenishing it faces compounding access problems as those clinicians retire.

## The Workforce Isn't Just Concentrating Geographically. It's Changing Shape.

The specialty mix of recent graduates looks almost nothing like the pre-2000 cohort. Nurse Practitioners make up **31.41%** of post-2015 graduates, compared to just 2.57% of pre-2000 graduates. Physician Assistants account for 12.61% of recent graduates, versus 2.34% of the earlier cohort. These two categories alone represent nearly half of everyone who has entered the workforce in the past decade.

The flip side is a sharp decline in traditional primary care. Family Practice represents only 4.52% of recent graduates, down from 8.98% among pre-2000 clinicians. Internal Medicine fell even further in relative terms, from 10.60% of the pre-2000 cohort to just 4.23% of recent graduates. In raw numbers, there are more Family Practice clinicians from the pre-2000 cohort (33,649) than from the post-2015 cohort (26,063), despite the overall workforce being far larger today.

That inversion has direct consequences for primary care access. NPs and PAs expand the total supply of clinicians, but their scope of practice, training, and distribution patterns differ from physicians. If the cities absorbing the most recent graduates are also the cities with the most existing NP infrastructure, the geographic concentration problem and the specialty mix problem reinforce each other. Underserved areas don't just get fewer new clinicians overall. They get fewer of the primary care physicians they were already short on.

The workforce pipeline has been fundamentally restructured. The question is whether the places that most need new clinicians are positioned to attract the kinds of clinicians who are actually graduating.
