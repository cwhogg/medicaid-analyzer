Between 2015 and 2024, binge drinking among women aged 45-64 rose 15 percent. Among men the same age, it barely moved. Those two trends, running in opposite directions at different speeds, tell a story about who is actually driving alcohol harm in America right now.

## The Gap That Closed From Below

Start with the baseline. In 2015, **18.1% of men aged 45-64** reported binge drinking, versus 9.2% of women in the same group. A near two-to-one ratio. By 2024, men sat at 19.0% and women at 10.6%. The gap narrowed not because men pulled back, but because women moved forward.

Men's trajectory over this period was essentially flat, with one notable dip to 17.1% in 2021 before recovering to 19.0% by 2024. Women's trajectory was different: a drop to 9.1% in 2020, a rebound to a peak of 10.7% in 2022, then a slight settle to 10.6% in 2024. The 2020 trough followed by a sharp rebound is the more consequential pattern. Women in this age group didn't just return to pre-pandemic levels; they exceeded them.

For clinical screening programs calibrated to catch binge drinking in younger men, this shift means a growing share of at-risk patients are walking through the door without triggering standard red flags.

## A Compositional Shift in Who Is Binge Drinking

The individual prevalence numbers matter, but the population-level shift is what makes this finding consequential for prevention infrastructure.

Women aged 45 and older represented **11.6% of all binge drinkers in 2014**. By 2024, that share had grown to 13.5%. That's nearly two percentage points of compositional change in a decade, measured across a pool of roughly 55,000 binge drinkers in the 2024 sample.

To put that in context: the traditional binge-drinking profile centers on young adults. In 2015, male binge drinking among 25-34 year-olds hit 33.1%, the highest of any male age group. Female binge drinking peaked at 20.3% among 18-24 year-olds that same year. Those groups still carry the highest rates. But the direction of change points elsewhere. Rates among younger cohorts have not grown the way rates among middle-aged women have, and the compositional math reflects that.

Every percentage point of share that women 45+ gain in the binge-drinking population represents a real mismatch between where alcohol harm is growing and where screening, messaging, and intervention resources are concentrated.

## What the Trend Line Doesn't Yet Show

The 2022 peak of 10.7% among women 45-64 is worth holding onto. It came two years into a period of documented stress, social disruption, and changed drinking contexts. The slight decline to 10.6% in 2024 could signal stabilization, or it could be noise around a plateau that remains well above 2015 levels.

What the national data can't answer is where within the country this increase is concentrated. The state-level breakdown of female binge drinking increases for this age group would tell us whether this is a broad national trend or a pattern driven by a handful of states with specific demographic or economic profiles. That data exists in BRFSS but wasn't captured in this analysis.

What the national numbers do confirm is that a decade of gradual compositional change has produced a binge-drinking population that looks meaningfully different from the one that shaped most of our prevention infrastructure. Women aged 45-64 now account for a larger share of binge drinkers than they did ten years ago, their rates rose while men's held flat, and the gap between the two groups is narrower than it has ever been in this dataset. The programs built to address binge drinking were designed for a different population.
