Food insecurity doesn't just mean skipped meals. It shows up in blood draws.

Among low-income adults aged 20 to 64, those with low or very low food security had a diabetes rate of **17.5%**, nearly double the 9.4% rate among food-secure adults at comparable income levels. That gap isn't a rounding error. It's a clinical signal embedded in routine lab work, and it persists even after restricting the comparison to people living below twice the federal poverty line.

## The Metabolic Gap Is Visible Across Three Biomarkers

The HbA1c difference is the headline, but it's not the only place food insecurity registers. Among low-income adults, the food-insecure group had a weighted mean HbA1c of **5.98**, compared to 5.74 for food-secure adults in the same income bracket. That 0.24-point difference sits squarely in the prediabetes transition zone, where small shifts carry outsized long-term consequences for kidney function, cardiovascular risk, and insulin dependence.

Inflammation tells a similar story. Weighted mean high-sensitivity CRP was 4.96 mg/L for food-insecure adults versus 4.38 mg/L for food-secure adults in the same low-income group. CRP at that level is associated with elevated cardiovascular risk, and the gap between these two groups exists entirely within a population that is already economically stressed. Food insecurity appears to add a distinct inflammatory burden on top of poverty itself.

Hemoglobin rounds out the picture. Food-insecure adults averaged 13.66 g/dL compared to 13.86 g/dL among food-secure peers. The difference is modest in absolute terms, but directionally consistent: across glycemic control, systemic inflammation, and oxygen-carrying capacity, food insecurity tracks with worse outcomes on every measure.

## Prediabetes Rates Reveal Where the Pipeline Starts

Diabetes doesn't appear overnight. The prediabetes numbers show where the chronic disease burden is being built.

| Group | Prediabetes (%) | Diabetes (%) | Either (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Food Insecure (Low/Very Low) | 23.3 | 17.5 | 36.5 |
| Food Secure | 21.5 | 9.4 | 28.3 |

**36.5% of food-insecure adults** in this low-income group already had prediabetes or diabetes, compared to 28.3% of food-secure adults at similar income levels. The prediabetes rates are relatively close (23.3% versus 21.5%), but the diabetes rates diverge sharply. That pattern suggests food insecurity is associated not just with entering the prediabetes range, but with progressing through it faster. For health systems managing downstream costs of uncontrolled diabetes, that 8.1-percentage-point gap in full diabetes prevalence represents a substantial and concentrated burden.

## Mental Health Follows the Same Gradient

The blood chemistry findings don't exist in isolation. Among women aged 18 to 29 with marginal food security, **28.5%** had clinically significant depression (PHQ-9 score of 10 or higher), compared to 16.2% of fully food-secure women in the same age group. That's a 12.3-percentage-point difference in a demographic that is already at elevated baseline risk for depression.

Young women with marginal food security occupy a particularly precarious position: not in crisis by administrative definitions, but carrying a mental health burden that rivals what clinicians see in higher-acuity populations. Depression at this prevalence rate affects medication adherence, appointment attendance, and the capacity to manage a chronic condition like diabetes. The metabolic and psychological findings reinforce each other in ways that complicate any single-intervention response.

The food-insecure group in the HbA1c analysis had an average income-to-poverty ratio of 0.93, versus 1.13 for the food-secure comparison group. Both groups fall below the 2.0 threshold used to define low income, but that residual gap of 0.20 points raises a question the data can't fully resolve: how much of the 8.1-percentage-point difference in diabetes prevalence is attributable to food insecurity as a distinct exposure, and how much reflects the additional income disadvantage within an already low-income population? The biomarker differences are real. Attributing them precisely is harder.
