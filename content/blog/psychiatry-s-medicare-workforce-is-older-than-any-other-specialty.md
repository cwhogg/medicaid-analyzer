New York is about to lose more than 500 psychiatrists, and the pipeline replacing them looks nothing like what's leaving.

That's the core finding from a Clinician Directory analysis of graduation cohorts across the psychiatric workforce. **578 of New York's 2,680 psychiatrists, or 21.6%, graduated before 1985**, placing them in the highest pre-retirement-risk bracket of any state analyzed. At the national level, the newer generation entering behavioral health is choosing counseling and social work over medical psychiatry at a ratio that raises real questions about clinical continuity.

## Northeast States Carry the Heaviest Retirement Exposure

New York's 21.6% pre-1985 share is the highest in the country, but it's not an outlier so much as the leading edge of a regional pattern. New Jersey sits at 18.9% (169 of 893 psychiatrists), Massachusetts at 18.8% (255 of 1,354). Three of the four states with the highest retirement-risk concentrations are in the Northeast, a region that also carries some of the highest psychiatric caseloads in the country.

California presents a useful contrast. It has the largest absolute count of pre-1985 psychiatrists at 499, but those clinicians represent only 14.2% of its total psychiatric workforce of 3,503. Scale provides some buffer. New York has no such buffer: its 21.6% share means roughly one in five practicing psychiatrists could exit within a decade, and the state's total workforce is less than half of California's.

| State | Total Psychiatrists | Pre-1985 Count | Pre-1985 Share |
|-------|-------------------|----------------|----------------|
| NY | 2,680 | 578 | 21.6% |
| NJ | 893 | 169 | 18.9% |
| MA | 1,354 | 255 | 18.8% |
| FL | 1,418 | 243 | 17.1% |
| TX | 1,591 | 226 | 14.2% |
| CA | 3,503 | 499 | 14.2% |

The practical consequence is straightforward: states like New York and New Jersey face a compressed window to recruit, train, or restructure care delivery before a significant share of their psychiatric capacity retires out.

## The Replacement Pipeline Is Dominated by Non-Physician Roles

Among all behavioral health clinicians who graduated after 2010, **Mental Health Counselors are the largest group at 34.89% of the total**, with 24,078 clinicians. MD-credentialed psychiatrists entering the workforce in that same period number 6,810, representing just 9.87% of post-2010 behavioral health clinicians. DO-credentialed psychiatrists add another 1,673, or 2.42%.

Clinical Social Workers (CSW-credentialed) account for 27.89% of post-2010 behavioral health entrants, with 19,245 clinicians. Combined, non-physician behavioral health roles make up the overwhelming majority of new workforce entrants. Physician psychiatrists, MD and DO combined, represent roughly 12% of the post-2010 cohort.

This isn't inherently a problem. Mental Health Counselors and Clinical Social Workers provide essential services, and scope-of-practice expansions in many states have extended what non-physician clinicians can do. But there are clinical functions that remain physician-exclusive: prescribing, managing complex psychopharmacology, handling psychiatric emergencies with medical comorbidities. The 578 New York psychiatrists at retirement risk aren't interchangeable with counselors, regardless of how many counselors enter the workforce.

## What the Numbers Don't Resolve

The ratio of 34.89% Mental Health Counselors to 9.87% MD psychiatrists among post-2010 entrants reflects a structural shift in who is choosing behavioral health careers, not just a temporary blip. If that ratio holds through the next decade, the workforce replacing retiring psychiatrists will be substantially different in clinical scope from the one it's replacing.

For states like New York, where nearly 580 psychiatrists could exit the workforce within a relatively short window, the gap between what's leaving and what's arriving isn't just a number. It's a clinical capacity question that no amount of counselor hiring alone can fully answer.
