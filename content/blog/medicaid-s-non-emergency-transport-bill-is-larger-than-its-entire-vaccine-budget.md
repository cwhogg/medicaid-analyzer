Across Medicaid's 2024 budget, non-emergency medical transportation (NEMT) consumed **$479,428,542**, up from $298,151,266 in 2018, a 61% increase over six years. That figure now rivals, and in some states exceeds, what the program spends on preventive care. The gap between those two line items tells a story about how differently states have chosen to structure their Medicaid investments.

## Massachusetts Spends $273 Per Enrollee to Get Patients to Appointments, $154 to Keep Them Healthy

No state illustrates the imbalance more sharply than Massachusetts. With 1,944,547 enrollees, the state spent **$531,366,580 on NEMT** against $300,442,170 on preventive care, a difference of more than $230 million. On a per-enrollee basis, that's $273.26 for transportation versus $154.50 for prevention. Massachusetts also filed 18,711,488 NEMT claims compared to only 3,447,146 preventive care claims, a ratio of more than five transport claims for every one preventive visit.

Virginia ranked second in per-enrollee NEMT spending at $187.41, with preventive care per-enrollee spending of $117.51 across 1,896,963 enrollees. Rhode Island followed at $166.19 per enrollee on NEMT. The pattern across these high-NEMT states is consistent: transportation spending outpaces preventive spending by a wide margin, both in total dollars and per enrollee.

For patients, high NEMT spending can reflect genuine access barriers, rural geography, disability status, or lack of reliable transit. But when a state's transport bill is nearly double its prevention bill, the arithmetic raises a real question about where clinical investment is landing.

## The States That Flipped the Ratio

Not every state follows Massachusetts's pattern. New York spent $1,128,585,897 on preventive care but only $187,298,020 on NEMT, yielding a preventive per-enrollee rate of $150.72 versus an NEMT per-enrollee rate of just $25.01 across 7,487,923 enrollees. Texas spent $973,980,703 on preventive care versus $88,289,366 on NEMT, with preventive per-enrollee spending of $170.66 compared to NEMT per-enrollee spending of $15.47 across 5,707,099 enrollees.

| State | NEMT Per Enrollee | Preventive Per Enrollee |
|---|---|---|
| Massachusetts | $273.26 | $154.50 |
| Virginia | $187.41 | $117.51 |
| Rhode Island | $166.19 | $75.39 |
| New York | $25.01 | $150.72 |
| Texas | $15.47 | $170.66 |
| Michigan | $13.76 | $151.19 |

New York and Texas are running the inverse model: high preventive investment, low transport spend. Michigan follows a similar pattern at $13.76 NEMT per enrollee against $151.19 in preventive spending. These states collectively cover tens of millions of enrollees, and their per-enrollee preventive spending rates are competitive with or higher than Massachusetts's.

The contrast matters because it demonstrates that the Massachusetts ratio is a choice, not an inevitability. States with large, geographically dispersed Medicaid populations have found ways to prioritize clinical prevention without running transport costs to the top of the ledger.

## A Six-Year Trend That Didn't Reverse

Nationally, NEMT spending dropped to $173,079,770 in 2020, the lowest year in the dataset, almost certainly reflecting pandemic-era appointment cancellations and reduced utilization. But the recovery was fast. By 2023, NEMT spending had reached **$481,103,040**, the highest single-year figure in the 2018-2024 period. The 2024 figure of $479,428,542 held near that peak.

Preventive care spending, by contrast, grew more steadily across the same period before pulling back in 2024. The two trend lines don't move together, which means the relationship between transport investment and preventive utilization isn't straightforward. States spending the most on NEMT aren't necessarily generating more preventive visits per enrollee.

Whether Massachusetts's $273.26 per-enrollee transport spend produces better health outcomes or access metrics than Texas's $170.66 per-enrollee preventive spend is a question the current data can frame but not answer. What the data does answer is simpler: in the highest-NEMT states, Medicaid is spending more to move patients than to prevent the conditions that require the trip.
