Medicaid billed 3,049,998 screening mammography claims under code 77067 in 2023, about 41% more than the 2,158,815 billed in 2019. Delaware, meanwhile, had 7,518 fewer screening beneficiaries over the same stretch, a drop of 41.4%.

Those two figures describe the same program in the same period. The national total says screening recovered and then some. The state breakdown says a few places never got back to where they started, and the 2024 numbers suggest the national recovery itself may not have held.

## The national total outran 2019, then slipped

Annual claims for 77067 (Screening Mammography Bilateral Including CAD) rose from 2,158,815 in 2019 to 3,049,998 in 2023. Spending followed: $109,655,438 in 2019, $166,293,469 in 2023. That's a 41% increase in claims and a 52% increase in dollars, so the average payment per claim rose too.

Then 2024 reversed part of it. Claims fell to 2,570,606, a decline of about 479,000, or roughly 16% from the 2023 peak. Spending dropped to $149,503,977. Both measures still sit well above 2019, but a one-year decline of that size is not noise in a program whose screening volume had been climbing.

For a researcher, the 2023 peak and the 2024 decline pull in opposite directions. Anyone who benchmarks recovery against 2023 will see a shortfall. Anyone who benchmarks against 2019 will see a surplus. The choice of baseline decides the story.

## Delaware and Illinois lost beneficiaries while others gained

Claim counts tell you how much was billed. Beneficiary counts tell you how many women were reached. On that measure, the 2019 to 2023 picture splits sharply by state.

| State | 2019 beneficiaries | 2023 beneficiaries | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Delaware | 18,159 | 10,641 | -41.4% |
| Illinois | 153,388 | 122,718 | -20.0% |
| New Hampshire | 7,146 | 5,723 | -19.9% |
| District of Columbia | 24,580 | 20,979 | -14.7% |
| Massachusetts | 289,530 | 375,948 | +29.8% |
| Kentucky | 111,656 | 149,928 | +34.3% |
| Hawaii | 17,892 | 25,011 | +39.8% |
| Washington | 72,206 | 102,985 | +42.6% |

Illinois is the largest absolute loss among identified states: 30,670 fewer screened beneficiaries. Washington added 30,779 over the same period, almost an exact mirror. Massachusetts added 86,418.

The split matters because a national average hides it. A woman enrolled in Medicaid in Delaware in 2023 was drawn from a pool in which screening reached far fewer beneficiaries than in 2019. A woman in Washington was in a pool where screening reached far more. The national claims total can rise while individual states fall behind, because gains in some states offset losses in others.

Records with a blank state field showed the steepest drop of all, from 18,382 to 8,040 beneficiaries (-56.3%). That group can't be assigned to a state, so it's hard to interpret. Still, it removed 10,342 beneficiaries from the count, a loss larger than Delaware's.

## A diagnostic baseline from before the disruption

Diagnostic mammography gives a useful contrast because its monthly volume is steadier. Claims for code 77065 (Diagnostic Mammography Including CAD Unilateral) ranged from a low of 10,301 in December 2018 to a high of 14,626 in May 2019 across the 2018 and 2019 months in the data.

That's a swing of about 4,300 claims between the quietest and busiest months, even in ordinary years. Any 2020 decline has to be judged against that normal variation, not against a single month. A one-month dip inside a 10,000 to 14,600 range proves little. A multi-month stretch below the floor of that range would be a real signal.

This is why the length of a drop matters as much as its depth. Screening is scheduled, and a missed cycle in a given year is not necessarily made up the next year. Women who skip an annual screen may simply wait until the next one, which leaves a window in which a cancer can grow undetected.

## What the gaps mean

The aggregate numbers look healthy. Claims in 2023 were 41% above 2019, and even the lower 2024 figure beats the pre-disruption baseline. But aggregate claims can't tell you whether the women who were screened in 2019 are the same women screened in 2023, or whether the mix shifted toward people who get screened repeatedly.

State-level beneficiary counts are the better test of reach, and they show real losses in Delaware, Illinois, New Hampshire, and the District of Columbia. In those places, fewer low-income women were getting screening mammograms through Medicaid in 2023 than in 2019. If that persisted into 2024, a cohort of women may be carrying cancers that haven't been found yet.

The open question is why claims fell from 3,049,998 in 2023 to 2,570,606 in 2024, and whether Delaware and Illinois took part in that decline or were recovering on their own track.
